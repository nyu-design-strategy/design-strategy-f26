# Course readings the skills refer to

Short summaries, in our own words, of the frameworks the skills lean on. These aren't substitutes for the readings. The skills will sometimes point you to one of these when a question comes from it.

The syllabus itself lives in Google Docs and is pinned in Slack; this file only summarizes the frameworks the skills refer to.

## Michael Porter, "What Is Strategy?" (HBR, 1996)

Strategy is not operational effectiveness. Doing the same things better than rivals gets copied. Strategy is choosing a different set of activities that fit together to deliver a distinct kind of value, and accepting the trade-offs that come with that choice. The test of a strategy is what it says no to. When a skill asks "what's true here that isn't true in every other industry?", it's asking for the trade-off.

https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy

## Roger Martin (with A.G. Lafley), *Playing to Win* (2013)

Strategy is a set of five linked choices: what winning means, where to play, how to win, what capabilities that requires, and what management systems support it. The part the skills use most is Martin's move for testing a strategy: instead of arguing about whether an option is right, ask **"what would have to be true"** for it to be right, then go find out. The conditions that would have to be true become testable premises.

## Clayton Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon, David Duncan, "Know Your Customers' 'Jobs to Be Done'" (HBR, 2016)

People don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in a specific circumstance. The unit of analysis is the job, not the customer segment, and the job has functional, social, and emotional dimensions. The competition for a job includes workarounds and doing nothing. When office-hours asks "who, specifically, has this problem, and what did they do about it last week?", it's asking for the job and its circumstance.

https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done

## Eric Almquist, John Senior, Nicolas Bloch, "The Elements of Value" (HBR, 2016)

A pyramid of thirty things customers value, from functional (saves time, reduces cost) through emotional (reduces anxiety, provides access) to life-changing and social impact. Useful for naming precisely what a solution delivers rather than saying "it's more convenient". It also helps see that different actors in the same situation may value different elements.

https://hbr.org/2016/09/the-elements-of-value

## Ben Thompson, "Aggregation Theory" (Stratechery, 2015)

On the internet, distribution is free and transaction costs approach zero, so value shifts to whoever owns the customer relationship and aggregates supply behind it. Aggregators win by having the best user experience, which attracts users, which attracts suppliers, which improves the experience. Useful when asking who benefits from the status quo and where power actually sits in a value chain.

https://stratechery.com/2015/aggregation-theory/

## Andrea Ovans, "What Is a Business Model?" (HBR, 2015)

A tour of what people mean by "business model", from Drucker's "who is the customer and what do they value" through Magretta's story-plus-numbers test to the business model canvas. The takeaway for this course: a business model is a hypothesis about how value is created and captured, and it can be wrong in specific, checkable ways. "Who pays?" is a business-model question.

https://hbr.org/2015/01/what-is-a-business-model
