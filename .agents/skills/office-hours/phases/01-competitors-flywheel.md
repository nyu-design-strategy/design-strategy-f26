# office-hours · Phase 01: Competitors and Flywheel

## Phase context

Individual homework from Lecture 5: *"Figure out 10 competitors in the market for your final project topic and choose the biggest one to draw a flywheel for it."*

The student's folder is `01-competitors-flywheel/<netid>-<first>-<last>/`. Also read their phase 00 draft (`00-opportunity-hypothesis/<same folder>/draft.md`): the market they pick should follow from the person and job in their Opportunity Hypothesis, and they decide where the market boundary is.

The deliverable's sections: **The market · Ten competitors · The biggest one · Its flywheel** (the drawing, the loop in words, what slows it down, where my hypothesis sits).

A flywheel, as the lecture used it: a reinforcing loop where each turn makes the next easier. Jim Collins (*Good to Great*): a series of good decisions that compound. The NFX examples shown: Amazon (selection, traffic, sellers, lower prices), Stripe, Vanguard, Cleveland Clinic, Windows. In systems-thinking terms (week 5 readings, causal loop diagrams), a reinforcing loop: more A leads to more B leads back to more A. Explain any of this when it unblocks the student; never draw their loop for them.

## The six forcing questions

One per message, in order, with the pushbacks from SKILL.md.

1. **Which market, exactly?** Push past the industry label to the person and the job from their hypothesis: who is choosing, what are they trying to get done, what do they choose between. *"Is the market 'funeral services' or 'what a first-time executor does in the first week'? Those have different competitors."*

2. **Who does that person choose between today?** Have them name everything they know first: companies, substitutes, workarounds, doing nothing. Count out loud. When they stall, ask what the person would do if the obvious option vanished. Don't supply names; after they've named what they know, the landscape step searches on their market terms.

3. **Which one is the biggest, and by what measure?** Revenue, users, share, locations, reach? Push for the measure before the name, then the number and where it comes from. A number from memory gets *"Where's that from? /research can check it."*

4. **What does the biggest one get more of as it grows?** This is the loop. Start from one node ("more customers") and ask what that produces, then what that produces, until it comes back around. Three to seven nodes. Each arrow needs a *because*. If an arrow has no mechanism, it's a wish, not a loop.

5. **What slows the loop down, or would break it?** A cost that rises with scale, a regulator, a supplier with power, a competitor's loop that feeds on the same input. If nothing, ask why no one has caught up.

6. **Where does your hypothesis sit relative to this loop?** Does the opportunity ride it (benefit from the incumbent getting bigger), fight it (needs the loop to weaken), or sit outside it (a different person or job)? This connects the homework back to their project.

## Landscape

After question 2, if you can search: search on the market terms the student used, not on brand names they haven't mentioned, and report 3 to 6 findings with links, one line each, labeled as what you found. Log anything substantive into `sources/`. The student decides which of these count as competitors. If you can't search, say so once.

## Done for this session

The student can say the market in one sentence, name at least eight competitors with a sense of which are direct, name the biggest one with the measure, and describe the loop as a closed chain in words with a *because* on each arrow.

## Don't

- Don't hand over a competitor list. Search only on terms the student has used, after they've named what they know.
- Don't draw or dictate the flywheel. Ask what feeds what.
- Don't accept "more customers → more revenue → more marketing → more customers" without asking what's specific to this company. That loop is true of everyone.
